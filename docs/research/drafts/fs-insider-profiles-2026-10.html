Who the insiders are in financial-services cases — edition 2

Numbers below are frozen at publication. The live, recomputed figures are on EVIDENCE (US | IN tabs). This edition ranks actor profiles by the insider's own employer sector. Edition 1 ranked them by the victim's sector and stays as published; nothing in it is rewritten here.

Ranking by the insider's own employer changes the edges of the picture, not its shape. 153 of the 155 cases with a financial-services employer were already in edition 1's victim slice. Six of edition 1's seven floor-clearing profiles still clear the 10-case floor. The ranking moves only where edition 1 had ties. Only 13 of 155 cases have reached an adjudicated or admitted posture. The employer field was asked only of rows whose victim was financial-services or unknown, so this edition cannot yet compare financial-services insiders with insiders employed anywhere else.

Read the table with two fills in view. "Current" is a default fill: the filing states no departure. It is not a tenure finding. "Unknown" means the filing names a party but no title. Percentages are suppressed below 10 cases and shown as n/a.

Findings

  1. F1 — Ranking by the insider's own employer keeps six of edition 1's seven floor-clearing profiles. The order changes only at the old ties. Ranked by employer, six profiles clear the 10-case floor: manager/current 33, executive/current 30, unknown/unknown 21, manager/former 15, unknown/former 15, executive/former 12. Finance/accounting/ops falls to 9, one below the floor. In the victim slice, executives and managers tied at 35 each; here managers lead 33 to 30. In the victim slice, unknown/former led manager/former 16 to 15; here they tie at 15. 153 of the 155 employer-slice cases were already in the victim slice. Consequence: a financial-services security or HR reader can keep using the edition-1 profile ranking. The employer framing removes outsiders and unknowns. It does not surface a new actor type. Caveat: the two slices are not independent tests. The 153 shared rows are 99% of the employer slice and 87% of the victim slice. Agreement between the two views is mostly the same cases counted twice. The 3-case manager lead sits on two default-fill rows. Net, the executive/current row fell by 5 and the manager/current row by 2 when 22 rows dropped and 2 were added. If 4 of the 14 employer-unknown rows resolve to financial-services executives the order reverts; 3 produce a tie. It is not a finding.
  2. F2 — Eight cases with a financial-services victim name an actor employed in another sector. Fourteen name no employer at all. Of 175 cases in which a financial-services firm is the named victim, 8 name an actor whose stated employer is in another sector: 6 cases with a professional-services employer (advisers, law, consulting, IT services), 1 public sector, 1 technology. Whether any of the 8 is proven is not in the export. In 14 of 175 cases the record carries no employer sector for the actor. The employer slice is therefore smaller than the victim slice mostly because of missing information (14), not because of outsiders (8). Consequence: vendor and adviser risk in this record is small and concrete. A legal or procurement reader should read it as "advisers and IT-services firms are the outsider class the courts have seen", not as a vendor-risk rate. Roughly one filing in twelve names a financial-services firm as victim but carries no employer for the defendant. The employer view is a subset of the victim view, not a correction of it. Caveat: 8 is below the floor, so no share is stated. Which function or employment-state rows the 8 occupy is not in the export. The executive/current row lost at least 5 and the unknown/unknown row at least 6 of the 22 dropped rows; which of those were outsiders is not stated. Whether the 14 are Indian pre-adjudication orders is plausible but unmeasured: no lane or posture cross-tab of the 14 exists.
  3. F3 — The employer slice holds 13 proven cases against 15 in the victim slice. The net loss of 2 falls entirely in manager/current. The manager/current row drops from 4 proven to 2. No other cell's proven count changes: executive/current 3, unknown/former 2, finance/accounting/ops current 2, unknown/unknown 2, front-office/former 1, technical/former 1. Consequence: two adjudicated-or-admitted-posture cases against a financial-services victim involve a manager who was not, or could not be shown to be, employed in financial services. For a legal reader, that is the concrete meaning of "victim sector is not employer sector": the proven record shifts when the question changes. Caveat: whether those two managers were outsiders or employer-unknown is not stated. The manager/current row also net-lost exactly 2 cases, which is consistent with, but does not prove, both dropped rows being the proven ones. 13 is below the floor, so no proven percentage appears anywhere in this briefing. "Proven" is a posture ceiling that includes acquittals: manager/current carries 2 acquittals and executive/current 1.
  4. F4 — No proven case sits in the departed-executive or departed-manager rows. As in edition 1, executive/former (12) and manager/former (15) are all alleged. All 27 sit at interim-injunction, quashing, complaint, bail, trial-judgment or unknown postures: manager/former quashing ×6, interim injunction ×5, unknown ×4; executive/former interim injunction ×5, complaint ×3, bail ×2, trial judgment ×1, quashing ×1. Consequence: offboarding-focused programmes cannot cite this record for a proven leaver harm in financial services. Every departed-manager and departed-executive row sits at a posture the strength column caps to alleged. Caveat: executive/former shows one trial-judgment posture with 0 proven. The export does not say why. The posture labels say nothing about jurisdiction; no lane or country column exists in this export.
  5. F5 — Coercion stays a manager story under the stricter framing. Among current-employee managers whose own employer is a financial-services firm, Coercion (MT012) is stamped on 10 of 33. Edition 1 counted 10 of 37 in the victim slice on the 2026-09-04 snapshot. The count is unchanged. Among current executives with a financial-services employer, Coercion is stamped on 3 of 30. Leaver (MT003) leads the manager/current row at 12, then Coercion 10, Resignation (MT003.002) 7, Personal Gain (MT005) 6. Consequence: investigations teams keeping duress on the interview checklist for mid-level managers have the same basis under the employer framing. Caveat: whether the ten are the same rows as edition 1's ten, and whether any outsider manager carries the stamp, is not shown. Motive stamps are enricher adjudications, not court findings. Manager/current is 31 of 33 alleged and is a default-fill row. Motive tags are multi-stamp, so they do not sum to the row.
  6. F6 — Finance, accounting and operations staff drop below the floor in employer mode. The row falls from 10 cases in the victim slice to 9 when ranked by employer (2 proven, 7 alleged). Edition 2 therefore states a count and no percentage for it. Consequence: edition 1's F2 line, that finance staff appear at nearly twice the corpus rate, is a victim-slice statement only. Do not carry it into employer-framed materials. Caveat: one case moved. The row sits at the floor either way; the floor rule, not the data, drives the change in what can be said. Edition 1 recorded all 10 as alleged; the 2026-09-05 rerun shows 2 proven in the same cell (see the snapshot note below).

How this was built

Corpus lane. Three deduped, verdict-gated corpus-industry runs of 2026-09-05 (9,227 documents → 2,943 enriched → 1,484 at schema v3 → 1,120 verdict-true → 1,108 method-bearing → 1,107 cases after story merge). 51 verdict-true rows sit below v3 and were never asked industry or employer.

Employer-sector slice. The actor's own employer sector is the additive field actor_employer_sector, backfilled on 2026-09-04/05 only for rows whose victim was financial-services or unknown. The export's table shows 183 rows with a named sector; its header says 184. This briefing cites the table. 45 rows were asked and answered unknown. 155 rows carry a financial-services employer. Employer slice = those 155 cases (13 proven). Victim slice = the 175 cases whose victim is financial-services (15 proven), recomputed on the same corpus.

Snapshot drift, stated plainly. Edition 1 froze the victim slice at 177 cases and 11 proven on the 2026-09-04 corpus. The same slice on the 2026-09-05 corpus reads 175 cases and 15 proven. That is a corpus-refresh effect, not an error: the overnight cycle landed new enrichment generations and the additive-field drain advanced, and both change which generation each row projects. By cell, proven moved finance/accounting/ops current 0→2, manager/current 3→4, front-office/former 0→1, technical/former 0→1, unknown/former 3→2. Edition 1's F5 noted one technical conviction the strength column did not count; that cell now shows 1 proven, which fits a posture re-stamp but does not prove one. Edition 1's numbers stay as published. Edition 2 compares employer to victim on the 2026-09-05 rerun only.

Review chain. One analyst drafted 12 claims from the export. Two independent refuters recomputed every number against the export and edition 1. 1 claim survived as drafted; 11 were amended; none was dropped outright. Where the refuters amended differently, this briefing uses the more conservative sentence. Two sentences inside amended claims were refuted and removed: one read Indian jurisdiction into quashing and bail posture labels, and one mis-stated which of the three technology-employer cases had a financial-services victim. The refuters also corrected the draft's arithmetic: unasked rows are about 892, not 937 (the 937 includes 45 rows asked and answered unknown); 183 of 1,120 rows (16%) carry a named employer sector, not 155; and the 3-case manager lead over executives was demoted from a finding to a caveat.

Limits

  • The employer field was asked only of financial-services-victim and unknown-victim rows. That is 183 named of 1,120 verdict-true v3 rows (16%). 153 of 155 employer-slice cases (99%) have a financial-services victim because of that scope, not because insiders never harm another sector. An insider employed in financial services who harmed a firm in another sector could not be found yet. One row in the export already sits outside the stated scope (two technology-employer rows with technology victims), so the scope line is a description of intent, not a proof.
  • The technology comparator is 3 cases and unusable. Only 3 cases carry a technology employer, because the field was never asked of technology-victim rows. Every edition-1 ratio against technology is victim-framed and stays that way. Nothing here says how financial-services-employed insiders differ from technology-employed insiders.
  • "Current" is a default fill. It records the absence of departure language in the filing. It never means the person was employed at the time of the act. The two largest rows, and the 3-case gap between them, rest on it.
  • 13 proven cases is far below the 10-per-cell floor. No percentage of proven cases is stated anywhere in this briefing, and no function is ranked by proven outcome. Counts only. "Proven" includes acquittals.
  • Lexicon bias. "insider trading", "trade secret … former employee", "computer fraud … contractor", the moonlighting cluster with "terminate", and "conflict of interest" are literal CourtListener queries. Rows those queries admit contain those phrases by construction. The IndiaCourts lane uses a different, local lexicon. Every count here reflects what two lanes collected, not what happens in the sector.
  • Posture caps strength. Complaints, interim injunctions, bail, quashing and writ stages never count as proven regardless of merit; settlements cap to alleged. Departed-executive and departed-manager rows are entirely at these stages. Posture labels never imply a jurisdiction.
  • Edition 3 needs a corpus-wide drain. A real employer-versus- employer comparison needs the actor_employer_sector backfill to reach the roughly 892 verdict-true v3 rows it has never been asked of. The unknown pool is where the technical (53 cases) and contractor (57 cases) rows live, with 18 proven between them — the rows edition 1 flagged as under-collected in financial services. At the measured 61 seconds per case on Nemotron, that is about 15 hours of sparky time at $0. It is an additive-field backfill, so no projection is rewritten. Whether to run it is a decision for the operator.
