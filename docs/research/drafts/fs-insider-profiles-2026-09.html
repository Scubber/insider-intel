Who the insiders are in financial-services cases

EDITION 1 (VICTIM-SECTOR FRAMING) · PUBLISHED 2026-09-04 · AS OF 2026-09-04 CORPUS SNAPSHOT (9,216 DOCUMENTS; 177 FINANCIAL-SERVICES CASES, 11 COURT-PROVEN)

Numbers below are frozen at publication. The live, recomputed figures are on EVIDENCE (US | IN tabs). Edition 2, ranked by the actor's own employer sector, will be a new dated briefing — never an in-place rewrite of this one.

This corpus cannot name the top ten insider threat actors for financial services. It can rank actor profiles — job function crossed with employment state, roles never individuals — and only seven of those profiles have enough cases to carry a percentage. Where a financial-services organization was the victim, the record is led by managers and executives who are still on the payroll as far as the filing says, and almost all of it is alleged rather than proven: 11 of 177 cases have reached an adjudicated or admitted posture. What follows describes what the collection caught, not a real-world rate.

Read the table with two fills in view. "Current" is a default fill: the filing states no departure. It is not a tenure finding. "Unknown" means the filing names a party but no title. Percentages are suppressed below 10 cases.

Findings

  1. F1 — Managers are the function most over-represented in financial-services cases. Among functions with at least 10 cases in every slice, managers appear in 50 of 177 FS cases (28%), about twice their corpus-wide share (159 of 1,156, 14%) and nearly four times their share in technology-victim cases (16 of 212, 7.5%). On role-stated bases the gap holds: 39% vs 16% vs 9.8%. Executives and officers go the other way: 50 of 177 FS cases (28%) against 37% corpus-wide and 48% in technology, where they are nearly half of all cases. Consequence: a bank's control set that watches only senior officers and privileged IT users misses the mid-level manager profile that leads its own sector's court record. Caveat: only 3 of the 50 FS manager cases are adjudicated or admitted; 26 sit at Indian quashing or interim-injunction stages. The manager/executive split depends on where the role normalizer draws the officer-vs-manager line on bank and NBFC titles; the two tie at 50 each. Industry is the victim organization's sector, so an outside executive in litigation with a bank counts here.
  2. F2 — Finance and operations staff appear at nearly twice the corpus rate; technical staff, contractors and front-office roles barely appear at all. Finance/accounting/ops staff are 10 of 177 FS cases (6%), about 1.8x the corpus-wide 37 of 1,156 (3%), and absent from all 212 technology cases. Technical staff, contractors/vendors and front-office sales together are 17 of 177 (9.6%), under half the technology comparator's 46 of 212 (21.7%); no one of these functions reaches the 10-case floor in FS (4, 5 and 8 cases), so only the combined figure is quoted. Third-party actors by employment state number 4 of 177, too few to state a share. Consequence: do not read the thin technical, contractor and front-office rows as low risk. Tellers, traders, relationship managers and vendors are under-collected here, not cleared. Caveat: the finance/ops count sits exactly at the reporting floor, all 10 are alleged, and 6 of 10 are Indian pre-adjudication postures. The collection lexicon shapes the gap: the trade-secret, IT-worker and laptop-farm queries pull technical and contractor defendants into technology dockets, while no query names a banking role by title and no query targets vendors in financial services.
  3. F3 — Half of the FS table sits on a default fill, so "current employees dominate" is not a finding this data can make. 90 of 177 FS cases (51%) are marked "current" only because the filing does not say the person left, against 40% corpus-wide and 38% in technology, where former or fired actors are the largest group (100 of 212, 47%). The two largest FS profiles (manager/current 37, executive/current 34) are both fill rows. 42 of those 90 "current" cases (47%) also carry an MT003 Leaver tag — a merged lexical-plus-LLM ITM hit whose aliases ("former employee", "resignation", "left the company") match document text, including the collection queries themselves. That rate is below the corpus-wide Leaver rate (59%), so the tag says nothing about tenure either way. Consequence: HR and legal readers should treat the current/former column as a statement about the filing's wording, not about whether the person was employed at the time of the act. Offboarding controls cannot be scoped from it. Caveat: "former employee" and "terminate" are literal CourtListener query terms, so departure language is partly collection-shaped. Indian bail, quashing and interim-injunction orders rarely state employment status and fall to the fill. No lane-by-state cross-tab exists in this snapshot, so the lane effect is plausible but unmeasured.
  4. F4 — Leaver is the leading motive stamp; Coercion and Personal Gain mark the sector apart from technology. Leaver (MT003) is stamped on 88 of 177 FS cases (50%; Resignation MT003.002 on 44, 25%) against 59% corpus-wide, and leads in nine of the thirteen motive-bearing profiles. Coercion (MT012) leans financial-services: FS holds 22 of the corpus's 65 Coercion cases (34%) while holding 16% of verdict-true rows, concentrated in the manager/current profile (10 of 37, 27%). Personal Gain (MT005) is 32 of 177 (18%). Technology shows Coercion in 2 and Personal Gain in 5 cases among its five largest profiles (a floor), while Recognition (MT024) appears in 14 of 212 technology cases (7%) but only 7 FS cases (count only). Conflicts of Interest (MT021) is 11 of 177 (6%); Revenge (MT023, 4), Resentment (MT007, 6) and Extortion (MT005.005, 3) stay below the floor. Consequence: the pressure pattern in a bank's own sector record is people being leaned on or paying themselves, not seeking credit. Investigations teams should keep coercion and duress on the interview checklist for mid-level managers, not only for junior staff. Caveat: motive stamps are enricher adjudications, not court findings; only 2 of 65 Coercion cases are proven corpus-wide, and the manager/current profile is 34 of 37 alleged. No query targets coercion, so that count is not lexicon-made. The Leaver count partly is: "former employee" and "terminate" are literal collection queries. Conflicts of Interest is seeded directly by the "conflict of interest" and moonlighting queries.
  5. F5 — Only 11 of 177 FS cases are proven, and none of them is a departed executive or manager. 11 of 177 (6.2%) have reached an adjudicated or admitted posture; 166 (94%) are alleged. That is the same band as the corpus (88 of 1,156, 7.6%) and technology (12 of 212, 5.7%); the gaps are a handful of cases across differently built denominators and are not a ranking. The 11 sit in four profiles: manager with no leaver language (3 of 37), executive/officer with no leaver language (3 of 34), former staff of unknown function (3 of 18), and fully unknown actors (2 of 30). None falls in executive/former (0 of 15), manager/former (0 of 13) or finance/ops (0 of 10). Five of the 11 have no known function. In technology, 7 of 12 proven cases sit in technical or contractor cells; FS records none there, but on 4- and 5-case cells that cannot support a sector difference. Consequence: a legal reader should not build a "proven profile" for the sector. Every departed-executive row is a complaint, interim injunction, bail or writ stage, which case strength caps to alleged regardless of merit. Caveat: "adjudicated" is a posture ceiling. It includes acquittals and admissions, so it is not a count of insiders found liable; manager/current shows one conviction beside three acquittals. One FS technical case carries a conviction posture the strength column does not count. Per-cell rates below 10 cases are not meaningful.
  6. F6 — Nearly three in ten FS cases never state the actor's function, so a ranked list of ten actor types cannot be built. 49 of 177 cases (28%) carry no usable job function, close to the corpus-wide 31% (359 of 1,156) and above technology (48 of 212, 23%). 18 of those 49 (37%) are still tagged former/fired, a state filled from departure language such as the literal "former employee" query, not from a stated title. The 30 with neither role nor state lean on Indian bail and quashing orders, where filings name parties but rarely titles. Managers and executives together account for 100 of 177 cases (56%), of which 94 are alleged and 6 proven. The technology comparator shows the same shape (executive/current 59 of 212, 1 proven), so this is a corpus-collection pattern, not an FS signal. Consequence: every named-function share above is a floor, not a ceiling. Which rows would grow if the 49 roles were filled cannot be inferred from this snapshot. Caveat: the role-unstated rows are almost all alleged (5 of 49 adjudicated). The 177 denominator is itself collection-shaped and no share here is a real-world base rate.

How this was built

Corpus lane. Deduped, verdict-gated corpus run of 2026-09-04 (9,216 documents → 2,938 enriched → 1,479 at schema v3 → 1,119 verdict-true → 1,105 cases after story merge). The corpus-wide baseline counts all enrichment tiers (1,156 method-bearing cases, including 51 pre-v3 rows that were never asked industry); the sector slices are v3-tier only.

Role normalizer repaired 2026-09-04. Earlier snapshots misfiled bank and NBFC titles; this run is the first on the repaired mapping. The employment-state default fill was not changed.

Victim-sector slice. Industry is the victim organization's sector as stamped by the v3 enricher. 177 financial-services cases; technology (212 cases) is the comparator; a 52-case industry-unknown pool was checked for contamination and is not folded in.

Review chain. Five analysts drafted claims across six dimensions (function, employment state, motive, proof, gaps, lexicon). Each claim went to three independent refuters who recomputed every number against the three source files and the collection lexicon in the CourtListener query set. 19 claims survived of 28. Nine were refuted, mostly for jurisdiction inferred from posture labels, percentages on sub-floor cells, or naming "embezzlement" as a collection query when it is not one (it enters only through the company-watchlist scope terms). This briefing uses the refuters' amended sentences.

Limits

  • Victim sector, not employer. "Financial-services insider" means the harmed organization is a bank, insurer, fund or NBFC. The actor may work elsewhere. Edition 2 will rank by the actor's employer sector once the actor-employer-sector backfill lands, as a new dated briefing.
  • 28% of cases state no role. Every function share is computed on 177 but rests on 128 role-stated cases. The unknown rows cannot be attributed to any function.
  • 11 proven cases is far below the 10-per-cell floor. No percentage of proven cases is stated anywhere in this briefing, and no function is ranked by proven outcome. Counts only.
  • "Current" is a default fill. It records the absence of departure language in the filing. It never means the person was employed at the time of the act.
  • Lexicon bias. "insider trading", "trade secret … former employee", "computer fraud … contractor", the moonlighting cluster with "terminate", and "conflict of interest" are literal CourtListener queries. Rows those queries admit contain those phrases by construction. The IndiaCourts lane uses a different, local lexicon and surfaces bail, quashing and interim orders by construction. Every ratio here compares what two lanes collected, not what happens in the sector.
  • Posture caps strength. Complaints, interim injunctions, bail, quashing and writ stages never count as proven regardless of merit; settlements cap to alleged. Departed-executive rows are almost entirely at these stages.
  • Comparator gaps. The technology file lists motives and postures only for its largest profiles, so tech motive totals are floors and its posture table is partial. The corpus-wide 31% role-unstated figure comes from a different lane base than the sector slices.
