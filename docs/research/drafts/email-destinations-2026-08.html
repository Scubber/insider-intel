Where insider email actually goes

PUBLISHED 2026-08-29 · AS OF 2026-08-28 CORPUS SNAPSHOT (8,939 DOCUMENTS)

Numbers below are frozen at publication. The live, recomputed figures are on EVIDENCE.

The email addresses insiders send company data to are personal, not professional. Of the 44 true exfil destinations found in the whole corpus, half are ordinary name-like webmail accounts, the machine-named inboxes are never the employee, and the "corporate" destinations are the insider's own side venture — not the new employer. The biggest pattern is not a destination at all: personal webmail as the office the scheme runs from.

Findings

  1. F1 — The funnel: 8,939 documents narrow to 44 true destinations. 828 cases quote at least one email address, yielding 2,969 case-address pairs. 68% of those are counsel and e-filing plumbing (ECF — the courts' electronic service lists). 428 pairs matched exfil keywords; every one was hand-reviewed; 44 are real destinations for company data. Consequence: raw address counts in filings measure the litigation process, not the crime.
  2. F2 — Personal webmail exfil is personal in character (22 of 44). Name-like account names, one-off self-forwards in the pre-resignation window — and zero auto-forward rules among employee cases. Consequence: the detection target is a manual forward to a name-like webmail address during the notice period, not a mailbox rule.
  3. F3 — Robotic destinations are never the employee (4 of 44). One is a nation-state exfil account; the other three belong to one gift-card fraud ring bulk-routing to disposable yopmail inboxes. Consequence: a machine-named inbox in your mail logs points at external crime, not a departing employee.
  4. F4 — Corporate destinations are the insider's own side venture (18 of 44). Dominated by the insider's own company domain — including lookalike and typosquat domains of the employer — not the new employer's domain. Consequence: watch for lookalikes of your own domain in outbound mail; the new employer's mail server is the wrong place to look.
  5. F5 — Encrypted mail shows zero employee exfil. All five proton-domain pairs in the corpus are attacker ransom contacts, an account takeover, an anonymous board complaint, and a pro se litigant. Consequence: do not build the insider program around the encrypted-mail scare — this corpus records no employee ever using it to move company data.
  6. F6 — Personal webmail is the office of the scheme more often than the warehouse. 65 pairs show personal webmail as the communications channel the scheme runs on — running a competitor from a personal account for months, invoicing from a lookalike domain — outnumbering true exfil destinations about 1.5 to 1. Consequence: monitor the channel, not just attachments leaving; the fraud's paper trail is in the mail body.

How this was built

A read-only, redacted scan of the stored corpus: domains only, local parts never printed — the site's roles-never-individuals rule applies to the analysis pipeline too. A context classifier separated counsel-service plumbing from mentions and keyword-exfil candidates; four reviewers then hand-read all 428 keyword-exfil pairs. Two independent redaction audits found zero unredacted addresses in report content.

Limits

  • Keyword classification leaked counsel-service traffic into the exfil bucket at a measured 38% before the 2026-08-29 precision fixes; the hand review corrected for it, but the automated counts alone are not trustworthy.
  • The corpus is collection-shaped: only litigated cases whose filings quote addresses appear, so absence of a pattern here is weak evidence of absence in the world.
  • Review worked from snippets of public filings, not full mailbox evidence — a destination's character is read from how the filing describes it.
